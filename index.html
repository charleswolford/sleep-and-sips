Milk supply and demand

Is enough breast milk being pumped to cover what the baby drinks, and how much of the rest is formula? A few things that help when reading the charts below: weeks start on a Monday and are labelled with that Monday's date, and partial days (the day he was born and the day of the export) are left out.

Milk pumped and milk he drank, ml per day

Each line is a smoothed average of the 7 days up to that date, so day-to-day noise doesn't hide the trend. If the purple line (pumped) sits above the green line (breast milk he drank), more is being pumped than he drinks. If orange (formula) rises while purple falls, formula is making up the difference. In the shaded early weeks he was also breastfed directly, which has no volume, so the bottle lines there undercount what he drank. Pumping only appears from the first logged pump. Tap a day to see the gap between breast milk pumped and breast milk he drank.

Time spent pumping, hours per day

The average time spent pumping each day, week by week, from the duration logged for each session. It doesn't include setting up, washing, or sterilising, so the real time is longer. Tap a week to see sessions per day and the typical session length.

Daily rhythm

Every day so far on one chart, so you can watch his routine take shape.

Sleep and feeds, one row per day

Each thin row is one day, with the oldest at the top and the newest at the bottom. Read each row left to right across the 24 hours shown along the top. Blue bands are sleep, orange marks are bottles, and pink marks are breastfeeds. A red mark at the left edge means that day's sleep log looks incomplete. Sleep was not logged in the first weeks, so the top rows show feeds only. Tap, or slide your finger along a row, to see details.

"Noon to noon" starts each row at midday, so a whole night sits in the middle of one row instead of being split across two. Over time you should see the blue night bands get longer and more solid.

Sleep

Days where the sleep log looks incomplete (under 10 hours logged) are left out of these charts, so a missed log doesn't look like a bad day. The Data quality section at the bottom lists those days.

Longest single sleep each day, hours

Each faint dot is one day's longest unbroken sleep. The solid line is the average of the 7 days up to that date. A rising line means longer stretches. The smaller chart underneath shows total sleep across the whole day, which changes much more slowly.

Total sleep in the day, hours

Time awake between sleeps, minutes

Often called wake windows: how long he stays awake from waking up to falling asleep again. There is one small chart for each part of the day, all on the same scale so you can compare them. Each point is the typical (median) time awake that week. The night chart shows how long he was up during night wakings. A break in a line is a week without enough complete sleep logs. Gaps over 5 hours are treated as missed logs and left out.

Feeding

Milk for his size: ml per kg of body weight per day

His daily milk total divided by his weight, averaged for each week. Bigger babies drink more, so this gives a fairer comparison over time than raw ml. Weight between weigh-ins is estimated on a straight line between them, and the chart stops at the latest weigh-in. Faded bars are weeks with direct breastfeeds, which have no volume, so they read too low.

How feeding has changed since birth

One bar per week. Each bar adds up to 100% of that week's feeds, split by type. Tap or hover a bar to see the number of feeds per day.

Growth

Weight by age, compared with the WHO growth standard for boys

The red line is the baby's weigh-ins; the bottom axis is his age in weeks. The green bands show the usual range of weights for healthy boys of the same age. The dashed line is the middle (50th centile). The darkest band holds the middle half of boys (25th to 75th centile), the next band the 10th to 90th, and the palest the 3rd to 97th. A centile of 25, for example, means about 25 in 100 boys of the same age weigh less. Following a band over time matters more than any single weigh-in, and your health visitor is the right person to interpret it.

Where an extra pair of hands helps

When the work happens through the day and night, from the last 28 days of logs. These charts go by who logged each entry, which isn't always who did the care, so use them as a starting point for working out who covers what, not as a scorecard.

Busiest hours of the day

Average number of feeds, nappy changes, and pump sessions in each hour, per day. The tallest bars are when an extra pair of hands makes the most difference. Tap a bar to see how many times each happened in that hour over the last 28 days.
